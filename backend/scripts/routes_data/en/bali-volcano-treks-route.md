Climbing a volcano is a trek on foot, but in most cases you can ride your own bike to the starting point rather than relying only on a tour operator's transfer: for Agung it's [Pura Pasar Agung](https://maps.app.goo.gl/UMRY6t5couqKRqj36) temple, for Abang it's [Likelike](https://maps.app.goo.gl/E3NFmoGL9Vq92Nm88) café and then on past the Warung Ariani viewpoint, and for Batukaru your bike can genuinely ride all the way to the [temple at the base](https://maps.app.goo.gl/QwGMbaZXsegXo5peA), where there's parking. The difference between the three volcanoes isn't just altitude — it's how early and how hard your day ends up being.

## Three volcanoes, three very different days

### Agung (3,142 m) — the highest and the toughest

Starting point: Pura Pasar Agung temple, arrival at the trailhead late in the evening:

> "On weekends we go up Agung. The route starts at Pura Pasar Agung temple. Usually this is where the so-called short route begins — just to the crater rim — and from there, to the actual summit, the trail gets a lot harder." — Ruslo

A detailed report from one of the climbs gives a level of timing detail rare for this kind of trek:

> "23:30 — gathering and briefing, 00:58 — start, 05:20 — summit, 05:45 — sunrise, 07:20 — start of the descent, 12:20 — finish. Over 1,700 m of elevation gain on difficult ground and rock. Plenty of dangerous sections, ladders, ropes. Bring proper food, light clothing for +8°C at the summit, a rain jacket, work gloves, at least 1.5 liters of water. One guide per three people, permit with guide — around 500k. Don't try to ride far after the climb — better to spend the night at the nearest guesthouse." — Alexey Biryukov

There's a separate warning about one specific descent through Kalibaru — "a dangerous stretch, a sheer wall going down, real risk from rockfall on the final section." Compared with Rinjani (Lombok), travelers in one discussion put it plainly: "It turned out the Agung route is more dangerous than Rinjani."

### Abang (2,151 m) — the route with a shaman and ropes

Agung's less famous neighbor, with a non-standard eastern trail and its own logistics:

> "We're going to catch sunrise on Abang with shaman Baga, climbing the eastern trail. It's a non-standard route: unlike the popular southern and northern trails (via Trunyan), this one has a view of Agung. There are stretches with a steep climb where you'll need to use a rope. Elevation gain: +1,300 m. Total (there and back): 9 hours." — Ruslo

The logistics for the day before the climb: gather at Likelike Coffee & Eatery (11:00) → the viewpoint by Warung Ariani, from where you can even see Nusa Penida on a clear day → Sibetan Rice Terrace → the Bukit Cemara viewpoint over Agung → check in at [The Mountain House](https://maps.app.goo.gl/hfV58jDpqr4fwnVw7) (a lodge at the base, check-in at 14:00, climb starts at 1:30 a.m.). The cost of the climb is 600–650k with the shaman (depending on group size) or around 400k with a regular guide, including transfer from Ubud, Canggu, and Kintamani. One participant put it honestly: "I did this route, it's genuinely tough."

### Batukaru (2,276 m) — the most accessible, with one caveat

Here travelers' opinions diverge, and we're giving both sides rather than picking the "right" one:

> "Was back on Batukaru recently. Found it prettier and easier than Abang — 3 hours up and the same back down. No guides needed, just sign the logbook at the entrance and drop 50k in the box." — a discussion participant

> "The start is here: you ride your bike all the way in, right up to the temple, where there's parking. At the entrance there's a logbook — sign it, and 50k goes in the box. At the peak there's a 360° view, but it clouds over after 10 a.m. Great to climb at sunrise — start at 2:00–2:30." — Vladislav Kravchenko

But another traveler warns the exact opposite:

> "Batukaru is some kind of cursed mountain — people regularly get lost there on flat, easy ground. First time, better not to go solo — if not with a guide, then at least with someone who's already done the route." — Mikhail

A third participant adds that, according to publicly available sources, a guide is actually considered mandatory on Batukaru. Given this direct disagreement within the same group of experienced riders, it's wiser to decide about a guide on the ground rather than rely on someone else's "you don't need one."

## Riding to the trailhead

The key detail for this article: the trek itself is on foot, but in most cases you can ride your own bike to the start of the trail rather than relying only on a tour bus.

- **Agung**: a paved road runs all the way to Pura Pasar Agung temple — the trailhead.
- **Abang**: you ride to Likelike café and then follow the route described above, past the viewpoint and rice terraces, to The Mountain House.
- **Batukaru**: "you ride your bike all the way in, right up to the temple, where there's parking" — the most direct bike access of the three volcanoes.

A night start (00:00–02:30 depending on the volcano) means part of the ride happens in the dark on mountain switchbacks — factor that into your choice of bike, and always check your headlight before setting off.

## What to bring

The gear is much the same across all three climbs, but it's worth quoting the specific advice from the travelers themselves rather than generic tips:

> "Good shoes, trekking poles, gloves, a comfortable backpack." — Ruslo, on Agung

> "I'd really recommend renting flat-sole trekking shoes — we were slipping constantly. I wouldn't bother renting a jacket or hat — we were peeling off layers within 15 minutes." — Diana Dreylikh, on a comparable climb on Rinjani (Lombok)

The second tip is an important detail: despite the cold at the summit before sunrise, it's easy to overheat during the climb itself, so layered clothing you can shed as you go is more practical than one heavy jacket.

## An easier alternative

If a "hard night climb" isn't for you but you still want a volcano view — [Gunung Adeng](https://maps.app.goo.gl/jmxXvQt1XbFjPtVN8) (1,826 m) is described by one traveler as a trek in light mode: "two hours up and two hours back, easy pace, nothing but forest along the way, 20k entrance." Less dramatic, but without the night start and ropes.

Mount Batur in Kintamani is another, more accessible option with an organized, regular route — details in a separate guide: [Kintamani: Sunrise at Mount Batur — Route, What Time to Leave, and What to Wear](/en/blog/kintamani-sunrise-route).

## Which bike to take to the trailhead

For the night ride to Pura Pasar Agung temple, Likelike café, or the temple at the base of Batukaru, what matters isn't engine power so much as a strong headlight and a stable ride on an unlit mountain road. [Honda ADV 160](/bikes?category=honda_adv160) or [Suzuki V-Strom 250](/bikes?group=motorcycle&model=suzuki_vstrom250) are a sensible choice for this kind of logistics — extra power in reserve on the climbs and a steadier ride in the dark than a light city scooter gives you. Remember the advice from one of the participants — "don't try to ride far after the climb" — plan to stay near the finish point rather than a long ride back right after descending on tired legs.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do you need a guide for the climb?**
For Agung and Abang — yes, based on participants' reports (a guide is included in organized groups). Opinions differ on Batukaru: some travelers climbed without a guide, while others warn directly that it's a mountain where people regularly get lost, and recommend going at minimum with someone who's already done the route.

**Can you ride to the trailhead on your own bike, rather than only by transfer?**
Yes — for all three volcanoes, travelers describe exactly that format: riding in on their own bikes, with transfer as an option rather than the only way to get there.

**Which of the three volcanoes is easiest?**
Based on participants' accounts, Batukaru is subjectively called easier and prettier than Abang (3 hours up, 3 down), though with the caveat about the risk of getting lost without an experienced companion. Agung is the toughest and longest climb (over 1,700 m of elevation gain).

**What do I do with the bike while I'm on the trek?**
Based on the trailhead descriptions, the temples have organized parking (at Batukaru, for example, right at the entrance, with a logbook). We haven't yet gathered a specific long-stay parking rate from our drivers.
